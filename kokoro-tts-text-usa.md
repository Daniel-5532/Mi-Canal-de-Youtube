### Stream block 1/2 (4695 chars)

```
Five hundred years ago, almost nobody in Europe knew it existed. Today, its economy produces more than 28 trillion dollars a year, its movies and music reach every corner of the world, and its military has bases in dozens of countries. This is the story of the United States: wars, revolutions, slavery, inventions, crises, and reinventions. Fourteen ninety-two... to twenty twenty-six. All in ten minutes. Let's go. Long before the United States existed, this land was home to millions of people and hundreds of different nations. From the great city of Cahokia, near modern St. Louis, which once rivaled the size of European capitals, to the powerful Iroquois Confederacy in the Northeast, and the Pueblo peoples of the Southwest. In 1492, Christopher Columbus sails across the Atlantic looking for a route to Asia and lands in the Caribbean. Explorers and conquistadors follow. The Spanish settle Florida and the Southwest; St. Augustine, founded in 1565, is the oldest European-founded city in the country. The English found Jamestown in 1607, and in 1620 the Pilgrims arrive on the Mayflower. But European arrival brought devastation: diseases like smallpox, against which Native peoples had no immunity. Their populations collapsed within just a few generations. Other European powers were here too: the French along the St. Lawrence and the Mississippi, the Dutch around New York. For a long time, nobody knew which flag would end up dominating the continent. Through the 1600s and 1700s, England builds thirteen colonies along the East Coast. Each had its own character. In the North, there was trade, fishing, and small farming. In the South, huge plantations of tobacco, rice, and cotton. And those plantations ran on enslaved labor. In 1619, the first Africans arrive in Virginia, and over time millions would be kidnapped and brought across the Atlantic by force. By the time of the Revolution, slavery was legal in every one of the thirteen colonies. It would become the country's greatest wound, and its greatest conflict, for the next two centuries. Meanwhile, ideas were growing too. Colonial assemblies, town meetings, and printing presses taught colonists to govern themselves, and that habit of self-government would soon matter enormously. After winning the war against France in 1763, Britain is deeply in debt, and decides to make the colonies pay. New taxes follow: the Stamp Act, the Tea Act. The colonists protest with a phrase that became famous: 'No taxation without representation.' Tensions rise. The Boston Massacre in 1770. The Boston Tea Party in 1773, when colonists dump a shipload of tea into the harbor. And in 1775, war breaks out at Lexington and Concord. On July 4th, 1776, the Declaration of Independence is adopted, written mainly by Thomas Jefferson. It declares that 'all men are created equal'... though at the time, that left out enslaved people, Native Americans, and women. The war is long and hard. But with George Washington in command and crucial help from France, the colonists win at Yorktown in 1781. Britain recognizes independence in 1783. In 1787, the Constitution is written, and it's still in force today. In 1789, Washington becomes the first president. Think about how improbable it was: thirteen small colonies, with no real army and little money, taking on the most powerful empire in the world. It was a gamble that could have ended on the gallows for every signer. The new country was small: barely the East Coast. But it grows fast. In 1803, the US buys the enormous Louisiana Territory from France for 15 million dollars, doubling its size overnight. Lewis and Clark set out to explore the West, all the way to the Pacific. The idea of 'Manifest Destiny' takes hold: the belief that the nation was destined to stretch from coast to coast. In 1845, Texas joins the Union. In 1846, war with Mexico begins, and in 1848 Mexico cedes California, Nevada, Utah, and much of the Southwest. In 1849, the Gold Rush draws hundreds of thousands of people to California. And all the while, expansion came at a brutal cost for Native peoples. The Indian Removal Act of 1830 and the Trail of Tears drove tens of thousands from their homelands, and countless died along the way. Steamboats, canals, and then railroads shrink the distances. Pioneers follow trails like the Oregon Trail, while cities like Chicago and St. Louis grow into hubs for trade and travel. By the mid-1800s, the fight over whether new territories would be free or slave divides the nation. In 1860, Abraham Lincoln, who opposed the spread of slavery, is elected president. Seven Southern states break away and form the Confederacy, and in April 1861, the Civil War begins.
```

### Stream block 2/2 (4498 chars)

```
It was the bloodiest conflict in American history: about 620,000 soldiers died. In 1863, Lincoln issues the Emancipation Proclamation, and later that year delivers the Gettysburg Address. In 1865, the South surrenders, and the 13th Amendment abolishes slavery. But Lincoln is assassinated just days after the surrender. Then came Reconstruction, an attempt to bring Black Americans into full citizenship, with new rights in the Constitution. But when it ended, the South imposed 'Jim Crow' segregation laws that would last nearly a century. For the first time, a war was fought with railroads, telegraphs, rifled guns, and photography, so people back home saw its horrors. The Union victory preserved the country, and redefined what it stood for. After the war, the country transforms. In 1869, the transcontinental railroad is completed. Electricity, the telephone, steel, and oil take off, driven by names like Edison, Bell, Carnegie, and Rockefeller, in an era known as the Gilded Age. Cities boom, and millions of immigrants arrive from Europe through Ellis Island, in view of the Statue of Liberty, which was dedicated in 1886. And after the war with Spain in 1898, the US gains Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines, and emerges as a power with interests far beyond its own continent. But the boom had a dark side: long hours, child labor, and dangerous factories. Workers organized unions, reformers pushed for change, and the Progressive Era brought new rules to tame big business. The US enters World War I in 1917, and its help tips the balance. The 1920s are years of prosperity, jazz, and parties... until October 1929, when the stock market crashes. The Great Depression begins: a quarter of workers lose their jobs. President Franklin D. Roosevelt responds with the New Deal, a massive package of reforms and public works. On December 7th, 1941, Japan attacks Pearl Harbor, and the US enters World War II. Its factories become 'the arsenal of democracy,' producing planes, ships, and tanks at a staggering pace, with millions of women taking the jobs of the men sent to war. The war ends in 1945 after atomic bombs are dropped on Hiroshima and Nagasaki. And the United States emerges as the greatest economic and military power on the planet. The New Deal gave the country Social Security, bank protections, and millions of jobs, and it permanently changed what Americans expected from their government. The Cold War begins: a decades-long standoff with the Soviet Union. There was no direct war between the two, but there were conflicts like Korea and Vietnam, the Cuban Missile Crisis, and the Space Race, which peaks when Neil Armstrong walks on the Moon in 1969. At home, the Civil Rights Movement erupts. Rosa Parks, Martin Luther King Jr., and thousands of others fight segregation with boycotts, marches, and sit-ins, winning the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965. The 1970s bring the Watergate scandal, which forces Richard Nixon to resign, and oil crises. In the 1980s, Ronald Reagan pushes tax cuts and a hard line against Moscow. And in 1991, the Soviet Union dissolves, leaving the US as the world's only superpower. Meanwhile, the post-war boom built suburbs, highways, and a huge middle class, and television, rock and roll, and Hollywood spread American culture across the globe. The internet and Silicon Valley supercharge the economy in the 1990s. But on September 11th, 2001, terrorist attacks kill nearly 3,000 people, and the wars in Afghanistan and Iraq follow, lasting for years. In 2008, a global financial crisis rocks the economy, and Barack Obama is elected, the first Black president. Then come Donald Trump's first term in 2017, Joe Biden in 2021, and the COVID-19 pandemic, which killed more than a million Americans. In 2024, Trump wins again and returns to the White House in 2025. Today, the country faces deep political polarization, fierce debates over immigration, tariffs, and artificial intelligence, and growing competition with China. Smartphones, social media, and now artificial intelligence are reshaping how Americans work, vote, and argue, and the world is watching how the country handles it. From colonies to superpower, with light and shadow: ideals of liberty alongside slavery and exclusion; innovation alongside conflict. The story of the United States is still being written, and 2026 is just the next chapter. If you enjoyed this, subscribe, and tell me in the comments: which country should we cover next?
```
